How do students respond when the price of university nearly triples but nothing is paid upfront, and does their response show up in early labour-market outcomes? I study England’s 2012 tuition-fee reform using administrative data that link school, university, tax and benefit records for entire cohorts. A difference-in-discontinuities design compares the discontinuity at the September school-entry cutoff for the first affected cohort with the pooled discontinuity for the four preceding cohorts. On-time university entry fell by 4.0 percentage points. Most of this decline was offset by delayed entry: participation within five years was 1.0 points lower and imprecisely estimated, although correcting for the final pre-reform cohort’s partial exposure to the reform raises this to a highly significant 2.2 points. Among entrants, attendance at above-mean and top-quartile institutions rose by 4.4 and 3.7 points, more than three times the increases across the whole cohort. Despite the fall in on-time entry, I find no statistically significant effects on employment, out-of-work benefit receipt or annual earnings four to seven years after expected entry, and the estimates rule out employment reductions larger than 1.4 percentage points. A large educational response to price need not translate into detectable changes in early labour-market outcomes
